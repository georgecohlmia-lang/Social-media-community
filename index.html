Your Social and Community Plan

One platform, where your audience already gathers. A 25-person engagement list and a commenting plan that respects the discipline of silence. Two or three communities you'll be present in — not build.

01 Pick your one platform

Not your favorite. Not where you have an account. The intersection of where your audience already gathers and what motion you can sustain authentically.

Run the audit, don't assume. Three evidence sources: (1) where the writers, editors, and operators in your category already post and argue; (2) where your existing readers actually came from; (3) ask three people who match your ideal customer where they read about your category professionally. Record what the evidence says, not where you'd prefer to be.
ExampleLinkedIn — the food retail / business-of-food audience consolidates there. Editors, operators, brand owners, trade-press writers all read and comment there as their default professional space.
LinkedIn rewards thoughtful long-form commentary. Twitter rewards reactive wit. Instagram rewards visual consistency. TikTok rewards vulnerability and pace. Which one is actually you — not which one you wish was you?
ExampleLong-form thoughtful commentary. The same depth my anchor pieces produce — the platform doesn't punish me for being a slow writer.
If your audience is somewhere your natural motion isn't, name that explicitly. The lesson's guidance: pick where the audience is, then think about how to make the motion work.
ExampleNone significant — both align on LinkedIn for me. If you're a B2C owner whose audience is on Instagram but who writes long-form, the trade-off might be: pick Instagram, accept that you'll need to translate your long-form thinking into visual carousels and short captions, and budget more time for that translation.

02 Build your engagement list — and your commenting plan

Two parts of the same practice. The 25-person list names who you're paying attention to. The commenting plan names how you'll engage with them — and what you won't do. You won't finish this list in one sitting; plan to revisit over a week or two as more names surface.

The 25-person engagement list

The editors, owners, operators, writers whose orbit is your audience's orbit — the most relevant voices, not the biggest accounts. Where to source the names: the authors of pieces you've cited, the central voices your platform audit surfaced, the comment sections your audience gathers in, and the peers you already know. Qualify each with one test: would a substantive comment from you, on this person's post, reach people you want to reach? If yes, they belong. If you just admire them but their audience isn't yours, they don't.

0 people — aim for 15 to 25.
ExamplesEditor at a food retail trade publication — covers the supermarket category you write about · Owner of an independent natural foods brand — frequent posts on retail dynamics · Writer at an industry analyst firm — publishes regular essays on category trends · Operator at a specialty grocery chain — posts about merchandising decisions
Your commenting plan

A 25-person list without a commenting practice is just a list. Here's where you decide what you'll engage with, when, and what you won't do.

What test will you use to decide whether to comment on a given post? Of the people you follow, several will post on any given morning. You can't engage with all of them. What's the criterion that gets you to "comment" vs. "read and move on"?
ExampleDoes my insider experience change the conversation? If yes, comment. If I can only add agreement or a generic point, read and move on.
Example10-15 minutes · Most mornings, first thing — before the inbox opens.
The corporate instinct is to engage broadly and supportively — to be "active" on social. The lesson's discipline is the opposite: stay silent more often than you speak. Name what you'll avoid, so the discipline is explicit.
ExampleNo "great point!" or other generic-agreement comments. No redirecting conversations to my own work or product. No commenting on posts where I have nothing specific to add. No posting about topics I don't actually have a view on, just to maintain presence.
03 Identify two or three communities

Not communities you would build. Communities that already exist. The function they serve is intelligence — where your audience tells you, in their own language, what they're struggling with and what they're considering.

How to find them

The finding method: ask your 25-person list and your best customers where they talk shop — the private Slacks, Discords, Substack comment sections, in-person dinners. Search your category terms plus "community," "Slack," or "forum." Watch where the writers you respect mention gathering. Then qualify ruthlessly: a community earns your time only if your actual audience is in it and the conversation is substantive. Two or three is the right number — enough for signal, few enough to be genuinely present in.

ExampleA ~40-person Slack of clean-label food owners · The comments section of a food-policy Substack where my audience consolidates · A quarterly in-person food entrepreneur dinner series in New York
The non-promotional discipline

The fastest way to be removed from a community — and to be remembered for the wrong reason — is to treat it as a distribution channel. Name what you'll commit to NOT doing in your communities.

ExampleI won't broadcast my anchor pieces in communities that didn't ask for them. I won't pitch my product or service. I won't redirect conversations to my own work. I'll comment, ask questions, offer specific help when relevant — and stay silent the rest of the time.
